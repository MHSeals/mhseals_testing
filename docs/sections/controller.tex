\section{The calibrated velocity controller}
\label{sec:controller}

Nav2 asks for a body velocity $\bm v^*$. The controller turns it into effort in
three parts: invert the model (feed-forward), cancel the day's drift (an
observer), and correct whatever is left with feedback on the measured velocity
$\bm v$:
\[
  \bm e = \underbrace{G_\text{eff}^{-1}\Big(\bm v^\star - \bm b_m - \begin{bmatrix}R(\psi)^\top\hat{\bm d}\\0\end{bmatrix}\Big)}_{\text{feed-forward}}
        + K_p\,\bm\epsilon + K_i\,\bm z,\qquad
  \bm v^\star = \operatorname{clip}(\bm v^*),\quad \bm\epsilon = \bm v^\star - \bm v,
\]
with $\bm e$ finally clipped to $[-1,1]$. $\operatorname{clip}$ limits the
request to the measured velocity limits, and the error is taken against that
limited request, so the loop never chases a speed the boat cannot reach.
$G_\text{eff} = G\,C$ is the gain the boat shows through the driver's decoupling
(Section~\ref{sec:mixer}).

\paragraph{The drift observer.} The characterization reports the drift on the
day it ran, but current and wind change. So the controller estimates the drift
$\hat{\bm d}$ continuously, in the world frame where it is constant. Each
odometry sample says how far the boat is from what the model predicts for the
effort it is applying:
\[
  \bm r = \bm v - \bm b_m - G_\text{eff}\,\bm e .
\]
Under the model the planar part of $\bm r$ is exactly $R(\psi)^\top\bm d$.
Rotating it back into the world, $R(\psi)\bm r_{xy}$, gives one noisy
measurement of $\bm d$, and a first-order low-pass filter averages them:
\[
  \hat{\bm d} \leftarrow \hat{\bm d} + \frac{\Delta t}{\tau + \Delta t}\left(R(\psi)\,\bm r_{xy} - \hat{\bm d}\right).
\]
$\tau$ (5\,s by default) trades noise against how fast a change in the current
is picked up. It is far slower than the boat's own response, so short
transients barely move the estimate. Because $\hat{\bm d}$ is kept in the world
frame and rotated into the body only when it is used, it stays right while the
boat turns. A body-frame estimate would point the wrong way after a half turn.
The estimate freezes while any effort axis is saturated, since the model is
not valid there. It is published on the controller's \code{drift} topic, and the boat test's drift line
compares it with the live velocity.

\paragraph{Integral with conditional integration (anti-windup).} The integral
\[
  z_i \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\!\left(z_i + \epsilon_i\,\Delta t,\; -z_\text{max},\; z_\text{max}\right)
\]
removes the steady error the model and the observer leave, such as a gain that is
slightly off. With the observer carrying the drift, $z_\text{max}$ can be small.
\emph{Windup} is what happens when the integral keeps accumulating while the
output is pinned at its limit: when the boat finally can respond, the stored
error makes it overshoot. Clipping $z$ bounds the damage. Conditional integration
prevents it: an axis whose effort is already at $\pm1$ in the direction its error
pushes stops integrating until it comes off the limit. The integral and the
applied effort reset when commands stop. The driver goes neutral then, and
the observer keeps learning from zero effort.

\paragraph{Stale odometry.} Feedback on an old velocity is worse than none. If
odometry is older than 0.3\,s the controller outputs zero effort and resets,
rather than integrating against a frozen measurement.

\paragraph{Why feed-forward first.} With a good model the feed-forward term does
almost all of the work, and the feedback gains can stay small and stable. If
the model is poor, the controller-steps procedure (Section~\ref{sec:step}) shows
it as large steady-state error or overshoot.

\paragraph{Why not Nav2, and why not the mixer?} The drift could in principle
be handled elsewhere. The mixer is the wrong place: it is calibration, and the
drift is not. Nav2 is too late: its controller plans with a kinematic model that
has no current in it, so it only sees the drift as position error after the boat
has already been pushed off the path. The velocity loop runs on measured velocity
over the ground, at the rate the drift acts, which is why the observer lives there.
