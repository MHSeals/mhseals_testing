\section{Least-squares characterization}
\label{sec:char}

To command a velocity we need to know what velocity an effort produces.

\paragraph{Effort, gain and bias.} \emph{Effort} $\bm{e} = (e_x, e_y, e_\psi)$
is the body command before the mixer, each axis in $[-1, 1]$: $1$ means as much
of that axis as the thrusters can give (Section~\ref{sec:mixer}). It is a
request for force, not a speed. What speed it buys depends on the boat's mass,
drag and props, so it is measured. The model is affine:
\[
  \bm{v} = \bm{b} + G\bm{e},
\]
where $\bm v = (v_x, v_y, \omega_z)$ is the steady body velocity, $G\in\R^{3\times3}$
the \emph{gain} and $\bm{b}$ the \emph{bias}. (This first form treats the bias as
one constant; the end of this section splits it into the part that belongs to the
boat and the part that belongs to the day's weather.) $G_{xx}$ is metres per second of
surge per unit of surge effort; an off-diagonal entry such as $G_{\psi y}$ is
yaw rate per unit of sway effort, i.e.\ how much asking for sway also turns the
boat. $\bm b$ is the velocity at zero effort: current, wind, or a thruster that
never quite stops. Once $G$ and $\bm b$ are known, the controller inverts the
model, $\bm e = G^{-1}(\bm v^\star - \bm b)$, to turn a wanted velocity
$\bm v^\star$ into effort (Section~\ref{sec:controller}).

\paragraph{Collecting samples.} The Characterize procedure holds a schedule of
efforts (each axis both ways at two levels, then combined directions), with
neutral between them. Every odometry sample $\bm{v}_k$ is paired with the effort
$\bm{e}_k$ held when it was measured, skipping the first second of each hold so
the fit sees the response and not the switching transient.

\paragraph{The unknowns, the data and the inputs.} Fit one output axis at a
time, say $v_x$. Its model is $v_x = b_x + G_{xx}e_x + G_{xy}e_y + G_{x\psi}e_\psi$,
one equation per sample. Stack the $n$ samples as rows:
\[
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} v_{x,1}\\ \vdots\\ v_{x,n}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{y}\ \in\ \R^{n}}
  \approx
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & e_{x,1} & e_{y,1} & e_{\psi,1}\\
                              \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\
                              1 & e_{x,n} & e_{y,n} & e_{\psi,n}\end{bmatrix}}_{X\ \in\ \R^{n\times4}}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} b_x\\ G_{xx}\\ G_{xy}\\ G_{x\psi}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm\theta\ \in\ \R^{4}} .
\]
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\bm y$ is what was measured: one velocity per sample.
  \item $X$ is what was commanded: one row per sample, the efforts held at the
        time, with a leading $1$ so the bias has a column to live in.
  \item $\bm\theta$ is what we want: the four numbers of this axis's model.
\end{itemize}
With hundreds of noisy samples and four unknowns, no $\bm\theta$ fits every row
exactly. Each choice leaves a \emph{residual} $\bm r = \bm y - X\bm\theta$, the
part of the measurement the model does not explain.

\paragraph{What is minimized.} Not $\bm\theta$: $\bm\theta$ is what we
\emph{choose}. We pick the $\bm\theta$ that makes the residual smallest,
\[
  \hat{\bm\theta} = \argmin_{\bm\theta}\ J(\bm\theta),\qquad
  J(\bm\theta) = \|\bm y - X\bm\theta\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(v_{x,k} - b_x - G_{x,\cdot}\,\bm e_k\big)^2 .
\]
Squaring makes misses of either sign count, punishes large misses more than
small ones, and makes $J$ a smooth bowl with a single bottom.

\paragraph{Deriving the normal equations.} Expand $J$:
\[
  J(\bm\theta) = \bm y^\top\bm y - 2\,\bm\theta^\top X^\top\bm y + \bm\theta^\top X^\top X\,\bm\theta .
\]
The transpose appears because a squared length is a vector times itself,
$\|\bm r\|^2 = \bm r^\top\bm r$, and $(X\bm\theta)^\top = \bm\theta^\top X^\top$.
Using $\nabla_{\bm\theta}(\bm\theta^\top\bm c) = \bm c$ and
$\nabla_{\bm\theta}(\bm\theta^\top A\bm\theta) = 2A\bm\theta$ for symmetric $A$,
\[
  \nabla J = -2X^\top\bm y + 2X^\top X\,\bm\theta
           = -2X^\top(\bm y - X\bm\theta) = \bm 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  X^\top X\,\hat{\bm\theta} = X^\top \bm{y}.
\]
These are the \emph{normal equations}. $X^\top X$ is only $4\times4$, however
many samples there are, and it is the same for all three axes (they share the
inputs), so one solve with three right-hand sides gives $\bm{b}$ and every row
of $G$.

\paragraph{What $X^\top$ means.} At the optimum, $X^\top\bm r = \bm 0$. Row $j$ of
$X^\top$ is column $j$ of $X$, so this says the residual has zero dot product
with every input column: with the column of ones (the misses average to zero),
with the $e_x$ column (no part of the miss still moves with $e_x$), and so on.
If any were nonzero, some input still explained part of the residual and the
corresponding coefficient could be improved. Geometrically, $X\hat{\bm\theta}$
is the orthogonal projection of $\bm y$ onto the span of the columns of $X$.
Multiplying by $X^\top$ also turns an $n$-row problem that has no exact solution
into a square $4\times4$ one that does. The entries are plain sums,
\[
  X^\top X = \sum_k \begin{bmatrix}1\\ \bm e_k\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & \bm e_k^\top\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  X^\top\bm y = \sum_k \begin{bmatrix}1\\ \bm e_k\end{bmatrix} v_{x,k},
\]
which is what the code forms (\code{rows.T @ rows}) before solving.

\begin{example}[one axis]
With one input, $v = b + g\,e$, the normal equations solve in closed form to
\[
  \hat g = \frac{\sum_k (e_k - \bar e)(v_k - \bar v)}{\sum_k (e_k - \bar e)^2}
         = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(e, v)}{\operatorname{var}(e)},
  \qquad \hat b = \bar v - \hat g\,\bar e :
\]
the gain is how much velocity moves together with effort, divided by how much
effort moved. The matrix form is the same idea for all inputs at once, which
also separates their effects when they were held together.
\end{example}

\paragraph{When the fit is not trustworthy.} $X^\top X$ is invertible only if
the columns of $X$ are independent, i.e.\ the samples excite every input: some
neutral, some surge, some sway, some yaw. A bag that only drove forward has an
$e_y$ column of zeros, so nothing in the data says what sway does. The code
checks the rank and refuses such a bag, rather than returning a
confident-looking but meaningless $G$. A tiny ridge term $10^{-8} I$ guards
against round-off. Holding each axis \emph{both} ways also matters: it lets
the fit tell bias (same sign both ways) from gain (flips with effort).

\begin{example}
If $v_x = 0.6\,e_x$, $v_y = 0.4\,e_y$, $\omega_z = 0.3\,e_\psi$ exactly, the fit
returns $G = \operatorname{diag}(0.6, 0.4, 0.3)$ and $\bm b = \bm 0$. In the
simulator, $e_x = 0.2$ produced about $0.9$\,m/s, so $G_{xx}\approx 4.5$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{How linear is it?} Not exactly. Prop thrust grows roughly with prop
speed squared, hull drag with speed squared, and reverse thrust is weaker than
forward. These partly cancel, so steady speed is close to linear in effort over
a working range, but $G$ is a straight line through a curve: it is best near
the efforts it was fitted at. Fit at the speeds you will drive, and expect the
velocity controller's integral term (Section~\ref{sec:controller}) to absorb the
rest.

\subsection{Two kinds of drift: the boat's and the weather's}

The bias above lumps together two things that behave very differently.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Mechanical} effects belong to the boat: an ESC whose ``stopped''
        pulse still turns the prop a little, a bent mount, a weak motor. They
        are fixed in the \emph{body} frame. Push the boat forward at any heading
        and a bent mount pulls it the same way relative to the bow. They change
        only when the hardware does.
  \item \emph{Current} and \emph{wind} belong to the water and the air. They are
        fixed in the \emph{world} frame: a current setting north sets north
        whichever way the bow points. Seen from the boat, it comes from ahead,
        then from the side, then from astern as the boat turns. And it is
        different tomorrow.
\end{itemize}
A single body-frame $\bm b$ fitted at one heading is therefore right only at that
heading and only on that day. Calibration should keep what is stable and leave
out what is not.

\paragraph{The model with both.} Let $\bm d = (d_x, d_y)$ be the drift velocity
in the odometry (world) frame and $\psi$ the heading. Turning a world vector into
the body frame is the rotation by $-\psi$,
\[
  R(\psi)^\top \bm d =
  \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi & \sin\psi\\ -\sin\psi & \cos\psi \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} d_x\\ d_y \end{bmatrix},
\]
so the model becomes
\[
  \bm v = \bm b_m + \begin{bmatrix} R(\psi)^\top \bm d\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + G\bm e,
\]
with $\bm b_m$ the body-fixed mechanical bias. (Current and wind also turn the
boat, but much less than they push it; the yaw row keeps only $b_{m,\psi}$.)

\paragraph{Still least squares.} $\psi$ is measured, so $\cos\psi$ and $\sin\psi$
are just numbers in each sample, and the model is still linear in the unknowns.
The axes now share $\bm d$, so they can no longer be fitted one at a time. Instead
each sample contributes three rows, one per measured axis, to one stacked system
with fourteen unknowns,
\[
  \bm\theta = (\,b_{m,x},\, b_{m,y},\, b_{m,\psi},\; G_{xx}, \dots, G_{\psi\psi},\; d_x,\, d_y\,).
\]
For a sample with effort $\bm e$ and heading $\psi$ the three rows are
\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l}
  v_x:& (1,0,0,\;\bm e^\top,\bm 0^\top,\bm 0^\top,\; \cos\psi,\ \sin\psi)\\
  v_y:& (0,1,0,\;\bm 0^\top,\bm e^\top,\bm 0^\top,\; -\sin\psi,\ \cos\psi)\\
  \omega_z:& (0,0,1,\;\bm 0^\top,\bm 0^\top,\bm e^\top,\; 0,\ 0)
\end{array}
\]
and the normal equations $X^\top X\bm\theta = X^\top\bm y$ are solved exactly as
before, now with a $14\times14$ matrix.

\paragraph{Telling them apart needs turning.} At a single heading the drift
columns $(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$ are constant: they are multiples of the bias
columns, $X$ loses rank, and no amount of data separates ``the boat pulls to
port'' from ``the current sets to port''. Only when the samples cover different
headings do the drift columns vary while the bias columns stay at one, and the two
come apart. The fit measures the spread of headings by the length of their mean
unit vector,
\[
  \bar R = \Big|\frac1n \sum_k (\cos\psi_k, \sin\psi_k)\Big| ,
\]
which is $1$ for a single heading and $0$ for headings spread evenly round the
circle. Below $\bar R = 0.9$ (roughly a quarter turn of spread) it fits both. Above it,
it falls back to the single body-frame bias and says so. The Characterize
procedure ends with a steady turn so this always has the headings it needs.

\begin{example}
A boat with $\bm b_m = (0.02, -0.01, 0)$ in a current $\bm d = (0.10, 0.15)$\,m/s,
sampled at headings all round the circle, gives back both exactly. Sampled only
at $\psi = 0.3$\,rad, the fit returns $b_{m,x} = 0.02 + 0.10\cos 0.3$: the
current folded into the bias at that heading, as it must be.
\end{example}

\paragraph{What gets saved.} $G$ and $\bm b_m$ are the boat, so they go into
the hardware configuration. $\bm d$ is the day: it is reported, with its speed
and the direction it sets toward, but never saved as calibration. The velocity
controller estimates it live instead (Section~\ref{sec:controller}).

\paragraph{Limits.} The largest measured speed per axis gives the velocity
limits; the largest change of velocity between consecutive samples divided by
their time step gives acceleration and deceleration limits. These become the
Nav2 velocity-smoother limits, so Nav2 never asks for more than the boat showed
it can do.
